<p>Three of the things that decide where a service business ranks in local search are remarkably ordinary: its <strong>N</strong>ame, <strong>A</strong>ddress, and <strong>P</strong>hone number, together called the NAP. They must match, character for character, everywhere they're listed: the website footer, the Google Business Profile, Yelp, the BBB, the Chamber of Commerce, the trade association, and dead directories from 2018.</p>

<p>It's tedious, and it's one of the most dependable basics of local SEO (the work of ranking in local search). Most service businesses with weak local rankings have a NAP problem they don't know about, and fixing it usually helps more than any clever tactic.</p>

<h2>Why Google cares about NAP consistency</h2>

<p>Google's map results (the "local pack") have to answer a hard question: which of the dozens of listings for "Acme Plumbing in Colorado Springs" is the business? When listings disagree, such as 1500 Main Street, 1500 N. Main, and 1500 Main St. Suite 200, Google has to guess which is correct, or it may count them as separate businesses.</p>

<p>To Google, a mismatch suggests either carelessness or duplicate businesses. Either reading lowers the listing's "prominence" score, which pushes it down the map results.</p>

<p>The fix takes care, not cleverness: every listing uses the same name, address, and phone format. When Google's crawler (the program that reads websites) sees them match, the prominence score climbs.</p>

<h2>What "exact" means</h2>

<p>It's stricter than most owners expect. To Google, "Acme Plumbing, LLC" and "Acme Plumbing" are different names, and "1500 Main St." and "1500 Main Street" are different addresses. "(719) 555-0100", "719-555-0100", and "719.555.0100" are three different numbers.</p>

<p>Choose one official version once and never stray from it. My standard is:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Name:</strong> the legal name on the business license, without "LLC" or "Inc." if customers don't use them. Pick one and keep it.</li>
  <li><strong>Address:</strong> in U.S. Postal Service format. Numbers as digits, street types abbreviated ("St" not "Street," "Rd" not "Road"), and city, state, and ZIP on one line. Leave out a suite or unit number unless it matters.</li>
  <li><strong>Phone:</strong> one format everywhere. I use (XXX) XXX-XXXX, with the area code in parentheses, because Google's structured-data reader handles it most cleanly and American customers read numbers that way.</li>
</ul>

<p>With the official version set, the check is simple: find each place it's wrong and fix it.</p>

<h2>The standard NAP check</h2>

<p>The check takes about an hour the first time and ten minutes each quarter after that:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Search the business name in Google.</strong> The first page shows the website, the Google Business Profile, and the biggest directory listings. Open each and confirm the NAP.</li>
  <li><strong>Search the phone number in Google, in quotes.</strong> "(719) 555-0100" returns every site with that exact number. Fix the legitimate listings. Old directories and "scrape" sites, which copy other sites' data, usually don't need attention, because Google already knows they're unreliable.</li>
  <li><strong>Check the major directories one by one.</strong> Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Yelp, Facebook, BBB, Yellow Pages, Manta, Foursquare, the local Chamber of Commerce, and the relevant trade associations. Log each in a small spreadsheet with the date you last checked it.</li>
  <li><strong>Check the website itself.</strong> Look at the footer, the contact page, and the schema (hidden code that describes the business to Google). The schema is easy to forget, and any mismatch with the visible text is a small warning sign.</li>
  <li><strong>Check old domains and subdomains.</strong> If an old website on a different address still loads, update its NAP or send its visitors to the new site with a permanent redirect (a "301").</li>
</ol>

<p>The result is a short log of every listing, its NAP, and what to fix. When I check for new clients, I usually find between three and 12 mismatches, almost always in old listings the owner forgot about.</p>

<h2>What schema markup adds</h2>

<p>Schema markup states the NAP to Google directly, so its crawler doesn't have to guess. For a local service business, the type is <code>LocalBusiness</code> or one of its subtypes (<code>Plumber</code>, <code>Roofer</code>, <code>HVACBusiness</code>, and so on).</p>

<p>It sits in a JSON-LD block (a standard format for this data) in the page's <code>&lt;head&gt;</code>, which visitors don't see. It lists the name, each line of the address, the phone in international format, hours, area served, and web address. Google reads it on every visit and uses it for the information box (the "knowledge panel") sometimes shown beside search results.</p>

<p>For NAP, the schema is the source Google trusts most. If the schema, the footer, and the Google Business Profile all match, Google treats the NAP as settled. If the schema and footer disagree, the schema usually wins, but the conflict counts against you slightly, so make all three match.</p>

<h2>What to do about old, wrong listings</h2>

<p>Almost every business has them: a 2017 directory entry with the old name, a 2019 Yelp review with a phone number since moved to a new line, or a trade-association page filled out by an office manager who left years ago.</p>

<p>How to fix each kind:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Listings you control (GBP, a claimed Yelp page, your Facebook page):</strong> log in and update them. Most changes take effect within 24 hours.</li>
  <li><strong>Unclaimed listings:</strong> claim them through the directory's "claim this listing" steps, which take a few minutes, and then update the NAP.</li>
  <li><strong>Trade associations and Chambers:</strong> email the membership coordinator. Changes usually take one to two weeks but stick once made.</li>
  <li><strong>Old directories nobody maintains:</strong> ignore them. Google already gives them little weight, so time spent there is poorly repaid.</li>
  <li><strong>Scrape sites:</strong> mostly ignore them. They'll copy the corrected NAP from trusted sources in time, and chasing them one by one never ends.</li>
</ul>

<p>The 80/20 rule holds here: the top eight to 12 listings produce 95% of the NAP signal Google reads. Get those right, and the rest mostly correct themselves as the trusted sources spread the right details.</p>

<h2>Why this is worth the hour</h2>

<p>The local pack is the block of three listings at the top of Google's local results, above the regular listings. Ranking there can mean 20 calls a week instead of two, and sitting just below it is worth much less.</p>

<p>Of the local SEO factors I know, only a complete Google Business Profile matters more than NAP consistency. It outweighs how fast new reviews arrive, links from other sites, and the content on your pages. It takes an hour to check and another to fix, and rankings respond within four to eight weeks. It's the dullest and most profitable hour a local service business can spend online.</p>
