WordPress runs about 40% of the internet. If you're a small business owner shopping for a new website in 2026, it's also the first thing most vendors will pitch, because it's what they already know how to sell.

But "popular" and "right for your business" are different questions. Here are the trade-offs of WordPress, the big hosted builders, the newer design tools, and custom-coded sites, for local service businesses that need a site that brings in calls, not an admin panel they'll never use.

## The short version

If you own a service business, most of the "WordPress vs." debate online doesn't apply to you. The question that matters is this: do you want to manage a website, or do you want to have one?

- If you want the admin panel and will use it, choose WordPress or Squarespace.
- If you'd rather email a person and say "change our hours," choose a custom-coded site with someone who looks after it for you.
- If you have a $20 budget and don't care about rankings, use a Google Business Profile alone and skip the website.

The rest of this post is the detail behind that summary.

## The options, in plain language

### WordPress (self-hosted)

The market leader. You, or someone you pay, install it on a web host, pick a theme, add a plugin for each feature, and log into an admin dashboard to make changes.

**Typical cost:** $20 to $50 a month for hosting, plus $100 to $500 a year for a premium theme and plugins, plus your time or $50 to $150 an hour for a developer when something breaks.

**Strengths:** It's familiar, it has a huge range of plugins, and almost any web developer will take on a WordPress project.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Security.** WordPress is the most-attacked platform on the web. In industry security reports, over 90% of hacked websites run on WordPress. Every plugin is a possible weak point, and missed updates pile up.
- **Performance.** Page builders (Elementor, Divi, WPBakery) produce heavy code. Typical WordPress sites load 2 to 5 MB of JavaScript on the home page, and Google PageSpeed scores in the 40s and 50s are common without aggressive caching.
- **Ongoing maintenance.** Themes, plugins, and WordPress itself need updates every few weeks. Skip them, and something will break or get hacked. Run them, and something else will break. It takes time every week.
- **Performance ceiling.** Even a well-tuned WordPress site struggles to reach Google's "Good" Core Web Vitals scores, because of the work the server does on every visit.

**Who should use it:** Businesses whose staff need to make a lot of frequent content updates themselves, and that don't mind managing the technical side.

### Wix

An all-in-one hosted builder with a drag-and-drop editor, on Wix's own platform, for $16 to $59 a month.

**Strengths:** It's quick to set up, needs no technical knowledge, and has a large template gallery.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Limited SEO.** Wix has improved a great deal, but its page addresses, speed, and control over structured data (schema) are limited compared with open tools. Agencies that specialize in SEO consistently report Wix sites ranking below similar WordPress or custom sites.
- **The site stays on Wix.** If you ever want to leave, you rebuild. There's no clean export, and your search history effectively starts over.
- **A monthly fee for as long as the site is online.** At $16 to $59 a month, 10 years comes to $1,920 to $7,080.
- **Design ceiling.** The templates are well made, but after a year most start to look dated, and you can't do much about the underlying patterns.

**Who should use it:** Someone who needs a site up in a weekend and doesn't plan to grow it.

### Squarespace

A hosted builder like Wix, with cleaner design and slightly higher prices ($16 to $52 a month).

**Strengths:** Better design defaults than Wix, and slightly better SEO out of the box.

**Weaknesses:** The same basic trade-offs as Wix: the site lives on the platform, doesn't move cleanly, and costs a fee for as long as it's online. It's faster than Wix but slower than custom code or a well-tuned static site (one built from ready-made pages).

**Who should use it:** Design-minded solo businesses that value looks over search ranking and are comfortable with an ongoing subscription.

### Webflow / Framer

The "modern builder" category: better design tools, cleaner code, and a visual canvas of professional quality.

**Strengths:** Their code is far cleaner than what WordPress page builders produce, and good Core Web Vitals scores are possible. Design firms that prefer designing visually to writing code often love them.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Cost.** Webflow's CMS plan starts at about $29 a month for the workspace, plus $14 to $39 a month per site. Framer costs about the same.
- **Lock-in.** Both use their own formats for code and content. You can't host a Webflow export elsewhere without a lot of manual work, and you lose the content-editing features entirely.
- **Wrong fit.** These tools are built for design firms and startups. For a six-page service-business site, you're paying for a learning curve and a subscription you don't need.

**Who should use them:** Design firms building for other firms, or startups that need a marketing site they can change quickly.

### Custom-coded static site

What I build. A static site generator (I use [Eleventy](https://www.11ty.dev)) turns templates and text files into finished HTML pages ahead of time, and I host those pages on a CDN, a network of servers around the world.

**Typical cost:** $0 for hosting (on Cloudflare Pages' free tier) and build tools, but someone has to build and look after the site. On my plan, that's a flat $175 a month, with everything included.

**Strengths:**

- **Fast.** Pages load in under a second, and scores of 95 to 100 on Google's speed test are the starting point.
- **Secure.** There's no server program, no database, and no plugins, so there's almost nothing for an attacker to break into.
- **Easy to maintain.** There's no weekly update routine. I publish improvements when they help, and nothing breaks overnight because of a plugin update.
- **Full SEO control.** You control every page title, every address, and every block of structured data.
- **One flat price.** Hosting and the security certificate (SSL) are included in the monthly rate, and your domain stays in your name.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Content updates mean emailing me** (or whoever built the site). Most service businesses prefer that to learning a content management system, but it doesn't scale for a staff of 10 who each write blog posts.
- **It takes a developer to build.** You can't download a custom-coded site for $0 and set it up yourself, and there's no template gallery.
- **I own the code while you're on the plan.** If you cancel, I help you move your domain to a registrar you choose, and the site goes offline at the end of the term.

**Who should use it:** Plumbers, HVAC companies, electricians, roofers, contractors, inspectors, landscapers, and other service businesses that care about rankings and speed and never want to think about the website after launch.

## How to decide

Ask yourself two questions.

**1. Who is going to update this site?**

If the answer is "me, regularly, because I want to log in and make changes," choose WordPress or Squarespace: Squarespace if you value design, WordPress if you need flexibility.

If nobody will, and you'd rather email someone once a month with small changes, a custom-coded site that someone looks after on a monthly plan is much better on every technical measure, and often cheaper over five years.

**2. Is Google traffic part of your growth plan?**

If your customers come through word of mouth and you don't care where Google sends people, the platform barely matters. Pick whatever is cheapest and easiest.

If you want to show up when someone in your city searches for what you do, speed and SEO control start to matter a lot. That points you toward custom code, WordPress with a developer who specializes in speed, or Webflow.

## The five-year total cost

For a typical service-business website, over five years:

- **WordPress with an agency partner:** $10,400 to $16,000 ($3,000 to $5,000 to build, $2,400 to $6,000 for hosting, plugins, and maintenance, and roughly $5,000 in developer hours for updates).
- **Squarespace:** $3,920 to $8,540 if you do it yourself, more if you hire help ($0 to start, $1,920 to $3,540 in fees, and probably a full rebuild by year five).
- **Webflow (built by a design firm):** $5,600 to $13,900 ($3,000 to $8,000 to build and $2,600 to $5,900 in platform fees).
- **My custom-coded plan:** $10,500 ($0 to start and $175 a month for 60 months), with upkeep and content updates included and no hidden fees.

Over five years, a custom-coded site is competitive with or cheaper than the alternatives, without the hidden costs of WordPress upkeep or a forced rebuild when a builder template goes stale.

## What about "just use AI to build a site"?

I get this question more every month. In 2026, an AI-generated site is a reasonable first draft. It isn't yet a finished, business-ready site for a small local business, because:

- AI-generated sites produce generic copy and layouts, which rank poorly because Google's systems are already tuned to spot them.
- Accessibility, speed, and phone-friendly layouts still need testing and adjustment that today's AI tools don't do on their own.
- Setting up hosting, DNS, the security certificate (SSL), analytics, structured data, and a working form service still takes know-how.
- No one answers the phone when something breaks at 9 p.m.

In five years, this may change. Today, AI is a good drafting tool and a poor finished product for a business whose income partly depends on its site.

## My recommendation

- **Single-owner service business** (plumbing, HVAC, electrical, roofing, landscaping, inspection) that wants to show up on Google and take calls: a custom-coded site that someone looks after on a monthly plan is almost always the right call. That's my business, so yes, I'm biased, but the math supports it over five years.
- **Solo creative or consultant** who wants something polished with little fuss: Squarespace.
- **Online store**, especially with a large product catalog: Shopify. It isn't in the list above, but it's the right answer for retail.
- **Writer** of a blog, a newsletter, or a publication: Ghost, or a static site written in Markdown (a simple text format).
- **Staff who need to collaborate** on frequent content updates: WordPress with a developer who specializes in speed, or a headless CMS such as Sanity or Contentful (a content editor kept separate from the site itself) connected to a modern static framework.
