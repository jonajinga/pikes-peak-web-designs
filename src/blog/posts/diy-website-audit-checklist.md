<p>Most service-business websites have problems the owner has never seen. The owner isn't careless; they just stopped looking at the site three months after launch. The site that looked great on handoff day isn't the one visitors see today. The fonts have shifted, the contact form is broken, the page takes three seconds to load on a phone, and Google's map results (the "local pack") can't see the home page at all.</p>

<p>Here's a self-check you can run in 10 minutes with only your phone and a stopwatch. It won't replace a full diagnosis, but it will catch the obvious problems. If your site fails any of these 12 checks, the failure is costing you leads.</p>

<h2>The audit</h2>

<p>Open your website on your phone, not your laptop, because the phone is what your customers use. Run the 12 checks in order.</p>

<h3>1. The five-second mobile load test</h3>

<p>Close every tab, open a fresh browser, type your web address, and start the stopwatch. In under two seconds, the home page should be fully drawn, with the top image showing and the buttons ready to tap. If you're still watching the spinner at five seconds, the site has a speed problem.</p>

<p>Why it matters: <a href="/blog/why-page-speed-matters/">Google's own data</a> shows that when a page takes three seconds to load, 32% of visitors leave before it finishes (the bounce rate). At five seconds, it's 90%. A slow site is invisible to the people who would have been your customers.</p>

<h3>2. The phone-number tap test</h3>

<p>Find your phone number on the home page and tap it. Your phone should open the dialer with the number filled in. If tapping does nothing, the number isn't set up as a <code>tel:</code> link. You're losing every mobile visitor who wanted to call but won't type a number by hand.</p>

<h3>3. The contact-form completion test</h3>

<p>Send a test message through your contact form from a personal email address. Do you get a confirmation? Does the message arrive in the inbox you check? If either answer is no, your form is leaking leads. <a href="/blog/website-costing-customers/">Most "broken" contact forms work fine</a>, but the messages land in a folder nobody watches.</p>

<h3>4. The "what does this business do" test</h3>

<p>Look at the home page for two seconds, then close your eyes. Could a stranger say what the business does and where it works? The headline and first paragraph have to do that job. If visitors have to scroll or click to learn your trade and your city, the site has buried the most important point.</p>

<h3>5. The address-on-the-homepage test</h3>

<p>Scroll the whole home page. Is your business address there, even in the footer? If not, Google lacks a clear signal that you're a local business in a specific service area, and ranking in the local pack gets harder than it needs to be. The same goes for your phone number and your list of service areas.</p>

<h3>6. The trust-element test</h3>

<p>Count the trust signals on the home page that show before any scrolling ("above the fold"). Trust signals include years in business, a license number, proof of insurance, and testimonials with names. Others are a photo of the owner or crew, an address, a Google reviews badge, a BBB rating, and certifications. The minimum is three, and the target is five. With one or none, the home page reads as a generic template, and visitors will shop on price.</p>

<h3>7. The single-call-to-action test</h3>

<p>The home page should have one obvious next step, or call to action (CTA), such as "Call now," "Book online," or "Request a quote." Two is fine, and five is too many. At 10, visitors can't tell what the site wants them to do. Pick one, make it stand out, and repeat it on every page.</p>

<h3>8. The image-quality test</h3>

<p>Look at every image on the home page. Are they clear photos of your work, crew, trucks, and job sites, or stock photos of people in suits giving high-fives? Visitors spot stock images at once, and those images cost you calls. <a href="/blog/how-to-photograph-your-work-for-your-website/">A simple phone-and-daylight setup</a> beats any stock library every time.</p>

<h3>9. The about-page mirror test</h3>

<p>Open the About page and read the first paragraph aloud. Does it sound like you? If it sounds like a template, or like a stranger at a marketing agency wrote it, it almost certainly was, and visitors can tell. The About page is the second-most-visited page on most service-business sites, so it should be the most personal page you have.</p>

<h3>10. The service-page-depth test</h3>

<p>Pick your top service and open its page. Does it cover the process, a price range, what's included and what isn't, common questions, and photos of the work? Or is it two paragraphs and a "Call us" button? <a href="/blog/how-to-write-a-service-page/">A full service page</a> is what ranks when people search your trade plus your city, and a two-paragraph stub doesn't.</p>

<h3>11. The Google-review-link test</h3>

<p>Does the site have a clear link to your reviews on your Google Business Profile? A line like "We have great reviews" isn't enough; visitors need a link that takes them to the reviews. A site that points to its Google reviews wins more business than one that shows hand-picked testimonials behind a wall. Pair it with a way to <a href="/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews/">earn more reviews directly</a>, and the loop closes.</p>

<h3>12. The "would I hire this business" test</h3>

<p>Pretend you're a homeowner with a problem your business solves. You've never heard of the business, and you found the site through a Google search. Read the home page, the About page, and the contact page. Would you call? If the answer is "I'm not sure," that's why your phone isn't ringing as often as it should.</p>

<h2>Scoring</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>10–12 passed:</strong> the site is in good shape. Check the speed and the contact form each quarter.</li>
  <li><strong>7–9 passed:</strong> the site has fixable problems, and most can be solved without a rebuild.</li>
  <li><strong>4–6 passed:</strong> the site is hurting the business, and a rebuild is the right call.</li>
  <li><strong>0–3 passed:</strong> the site is losing leads every week. A rebuild pays for itself the day it goes live.</li>
</ul>
