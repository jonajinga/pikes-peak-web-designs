<p>I'll be upfront: I have a strong opinion on this topic, and it shows in every website I build. But I also want you to have the full picture, so you can make an informed decision, even if that decision is not to work with me.</p>

<p>WordPress runs over 40% of all websites, more than any other platform in the world. So why don't I build on it?</p>

<p>The answer comes down to trade-offs that matter to any small service business deciding where to spend its money.</p>

<h2>What WordPress Is Good At</h2>

<p>WordPress deserves credit for some clear advantages:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>You can update content yourself.</strong> If you want to change your prices, add a service page, or write a blog post, most WordPress setups make that reasonably easy for non-technical users.</li>
  <li><strong>It has a huge ecosystem.</strong> There are thousands of plugins (add-ons) for almost anything you can imagine, from online stores and booking systems to contact forms and membership areas. If it exists on the web, there's probably a WordPress plugin for it.</li>
  <li><strong>Many web developers know it.</strong> If you ever switch web designers, finding someone who knows WordPress is easy.</li>
</ul>

<p>If you run a news site, an online store, or a platform with complex content needs, WordPress may well be your best option.</p>

<h2>Where WordPress Falls Short for Service Businesses</h2>

<p>For a local plumber, HVAC company, electrician, or contractor, WordPress has several serious problems.</p>

<h3>Speed</h3>
<p>WordPress is slow by design. At its core is a content management system that pulls each page from a database and loads dozens of files on every visit. Add a popular page builder such as Elementor or Divi, plus 15 plugins for SEO, security, and contact forms, and the site might score 35 to 50 on Google's speed test. That's a failing grade, and it hurts both your search rankings and your bounce rate (the share of visitors who leave right away).</p>

<p>Custom-coded sites, built with clean HTML and CSS, routinely score 95 to 100. It isn't close.</p>

<h3>Security</h3>
<p>WordPress is the most hacked platform on the internet, not because it's inherently insecure, but because its popularity makes it a constant target. Outdated plugins, unpatched themes, and password-guessing attacks hit tens of thousands of WordPress sites every day.</p>

<p>A neglected WordPress site is a liability, and many owners don't know theirs is neglected, because nobody is watching it.</p>

<h3>Ongoing Maintenance</h3>
<p>A WordPress site needs regular upkeep to keep working and stay secure: core updates, plugin updates, backups, and security monitoring. Most small businesses either pay someone for this or let it slide. When it slides, the site wears down: plugins break, security holes open, and pages get slower.</p>

<p>A custom-coded site made of ready-made pages, with no database behind it, has none of these problems. There are no plugins to maintain and no WordPress core to update, so it keeps running.</p>

<h3>Generic Appearance</h3>
<p>WordPress sites, even "custom" ones, are almost always built on themes: pre-designed templates adjusted with your colors, logo, and content. Experienced users can usually spot a WordPress theme. More important, themed sites often look alike across businesses, which makes it harder to stand out.</p>

<p>A fully custom site is designed from scratch for your business, with no template and nothing borrowed from a thousand other websites.</p>

<h2>What "Custom-Coded" Means</h2>

<p>When I say custom-coded, I mean a site written directly in HTML, CSS, and a little JavaScript, the basic languages of the web. There's no platform in between, no database, and no content management system. The code that runs in your visitor's browser was written for your business.</p>

<p>This approach produces sites that are:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Extremely fast</strong>, because there's no content management system, no database lookups, and no plugin weight</li>
  <li><strong>Secure by design</strong>, because there's nothing to hack: no login page, no database, and no WordPress admin</li>
  <li><strong>Built for search</strong>, because every part of the code can be written for search performance</li>
  <li><strong>Unique to your business</strong>, because each one is designed from the ground up rather than adapted from a template</li>
</ul>

<p>This is the most common pushback, and it's fair: WordPress's editing screen is easier for non-technical users than HTML.</p>

<p>But how often do you need to change your website? For most contractors and service businesses, the answer is a few times a year at most. You're not a news outlet, and your services don't change every week.</p>

<p>That's faster and less error-prone than logging into a WordPress dashboard and trying to work out why the layout broke after you clicked "save."</p>
