<p>Every service business owner with a struggling website asks the same question at some point: "Should I just fix what I have, or rebuild from scratch?" The answer, like most real-world business questions, is "it depends" — but it depends on a small number of specific factors that can be checked in under five minutes.</p>

<p>Most rebuild-versus-patch advice on the open web is bad. It is either written by an agency that wants to sell a rebuild ("rebuild!"), a freelancer who wants to sell a patch ("patch!"), or a forum user who has done neither and is repeating something they read. This post is the version that just works through the actual decision logic.</p>

<h2>Four questions</h2>

<p>Answer each one yes or no. Add up the yeses. The answer is at the bottom.</p>

<h3>1. Is the underlying platform still healthy?</h3>

<p>If your site is on WordPress with a theme that has not been updated in two years, on an old version of PHP, on a hosting plan that has been deprecated, on a Wix or Squarespace tier that is being sunsetted — the platform itself is the problem. Patching a site on a dying platform is throwing money at a foundation that is going to crack regardless.</p>

<p>The question to actually answer: <em>can I still log into the admin and make changes without something breaking?</em> If yes, the platform is still healthy. If no, the platform is the issue, not the site.</p>

<h3>2. Does the site load in under three seconds on mobile?</h3>

<p>Open <a href="https://pagespeed.web.dev/" rel="noopener">pagespeed.web.dev</a>, paste your URL, run the test. The mobile score should be over 70. The Largest Contentful Paint should be under 2.5 seconds.</p>

<p>If the score is under 50 or the LCP is over 4 seconds, the site has a fundamental performance problem. Sometimes it is fixable (compress images, drop a few plugins). Often it is structural — the theme is heavy, the platform is slow, the entire stack is the problem. <a href="/blog/why-page-speed-matters/">A separate post</a> covers why page speed is the single most important conversion factor on a service-business site.</p>

<h3>3. Does the design still match the business?</h3>

<p>Look at your homepage with a fresh eye. Does it represent the business as it is today, or as it was three years ago? Have you added services? Dropped services? Changed your service area? Updated pricing? Changed your branding even slightly?</p>

<p>A site whose design no longer matches the business is one that needs more than a patch — every page on it has to be rewritten anyway. At that point a rebuild costs no more time than a thorough patch and produces a far better result.</p>

<h3>4. Are you still in love with the underlying content?</h3>

<p>Open three of your service pages. Read them. Are they pages you are proud to send to a high-stakes potential customer? Or are they pages you wince at because you wrote them in a hurry and never came back?</p>

<p>If the content is solid, a patch can fix the rest. If the content needs a complete rewrite anyway, the rebuild is the only path that does not waste effort — there is no point patching the layout around copy that is itself going to be rewritten.</p>

<h2>The answer</h2>

<p>Add up the yeses to the four questions above.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>4 yeses:</strong> patch. The site is fundamentally healthy. Whatever is wrong is fixable in a one-off project. A freelancer or a few hours of agency time is the right path.</li>
  <li><strong>3 yeses:</strong> patch carefully. The site is mostly healthy but the no-answer is a real problem. Fix that specific thing first; if it does not fix everything, escalate to a rebuild.</li>
  <li><strong>2 yeses:</strong> rebuild. Two of the four foundations are broken. Patching costs nearly as much as rebuilding and lasts a quarter as long. The rebuild is the only path that ends well.</li>
  <li><strong>0–1 yeses:</strong> rebuild without question. The site is not salvageable in any economically sane way. Every dollar spent patching is a dollar that does not return to the business.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The patch path, when it is right</h2>

<p>If the four-question framework returned "patch," here is the honest playbook:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Run the <a href="/blog/diy-website-audit-checklist/">12-point self-audit</a></strong> first. It will tell you exactly what is broken.</li>
  <li><strong>Fix the contact form first.</strong> Submit a real test message. Confirm the email arrives in the right inbox. Most "broken" contact forms are silently routing to a folder nobody monitors.</li>
  <li><strong>Compress every image.</strong> Most slow sites are slow because the photographer's RAW file got uploaded as a 4MB JPEG. <a href="https://squoosh.app" rel="noopener">Squoosh</a> handles this in five minutes.</li>
  <li><strong>Add or fix the local-SEO basics.</strong> Address visible on every page, phone number visible, service area named, schema markup correct. The <a href="/blog/google-business-profile-quarterly-checklist/">Google Business Profile checklist</a> is the right starting point.</li>
  <li><strong>Update the homepage headline.</strong> The first sentence has to name the trade, the city, and one specific value proposition. Generic taglines do not rank and do not convert.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The rebuild path, when it is right</h2>

<p>If the framework returned "rebuild," the path is simpler:</p>

  <li>Run the build. Two to three weeks is the realistic timeline for a careful build of a typical service business site.</li>
</ol>
