<p>Every owner with a struggling website eventually asks, "Should I fix what I have, or rebuild from scratch?" The answer depends on a few specific things you can check in under five minutes.</p>

<p>Most advice on this comes from someone with something to sell: an agency selling rebuilds, a freelancer selling patches, or a forum poster repeating what they read. This post works through the decision itself.</p>

<h2>Four questions</h2>

<p>Answer each one yes or no and count the yeses. The verdict is at the bottom.</p>

<h3>1. Is the underlying platform still healthy?</h3>

<p>Warning signs include a WordPress theme not updated in two years, an old version of PHP (the code WordPress runs on), a retired hosting plan, or a Wix or Squarespace plan being phased out. Any of these means the platform is the problem, and patching the site spends money on a foundation that will crack anyway.</p>

<p>The question to answer is this: <em>can I still log into the admin and make changes without something breaking?</em> If yes, the platform is healthy. If no, the platform is the issue, not the site.</p>

<h3>2. Does the site load in under three seconds on mobile?</h3>

<p>Open <a href="https://pagespeed.web.dev/" rel="noopener">pagespeed.web.dev</a>, paste your address, and run the test. The phone score should be over 70, and the Largest Contentful Paint (LCP, how long the main content takes to appear) should be under 2.5 seconds.</p>

<p>A score under 50 or an LCP over 4 seconds means a deep speed problem. Sometimes you can fix it by compressing images or dropping a few plugins. Often it's built in: the theme is heavy, the platform is slow, or the whole setup is the problem. <a href="/blog/why-page-speed-matters/">A separate post</a> explains why speed matters more than anything else in turning visitors into calls.</p>

<h3>3. Does the design still match the business?</h3>

<p>Does your homepage show the business as it is today or as it was three years ago? Have you added or dropped services, changed your service area, updated prices, or adjusted your branding, even slightly?</p>

<p>If the design no longer fits, every page needs rewriting anyway. A rebuild then takes no more time than a thorough patch and gives a far better result.</p>

<h3>4. Are you still in love with the underlying content?</h3>

<p>Read three of your service pages. Would you proudly send them to an important prospect, or do you wince because you wrote them in a hurry and never came back?</p>

<p>If the content is solid, a patch can fix the rest. If it needs a full rewrite, rebuild, because there's no point fixing the layout around text you're about to replace.</p>

<h2>The answer</h2>

<p>Count your yeses.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>4 yeses:</strong> patch. The site is healthy, and whatever's wrong can be fixed in one small project. A freelancer or a few hours of agency time is the right path.</li>
  <li><strong>3 yeses:</strong> patch carefully. The site is mostly healthy, but the "no" is a serious problem. Fix that first, and if it doesn't solve everything, move to a rebuild.</li>
  <li><strong>2 yeses:</strong> rebuild. Two of the four foundations are broken, so a patch costs nearly as much as a rebuild and lasts a quarter as long.</li>
  <li><strong>0–1 yeses:</strong> rebuild without question. No patch makes financial sense, and every dollar spent on one is lost to the business.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The patch path, when it is right</h2>

<p>If the test says "patch," here's the plan:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Work through the <a href="/blog/diy-website-audit-checklist/">12-point website checklist</a></strong> first. It shows exactly what's broken.</li>
  <li><strong>Fix the contact form next.</strong> Send a test message and confirm it reaches the right inbox. Most "broken" forms are sending messages to a folder nobody checks.</li>
  <li><strong>Compress every image.</strong> Most slow sites are slow because a photographer's RAW file was uploaded as a 4MB JPEG. <a href="https://squoosh.app" rel="noopener">Squoosh</a>, a free tool, fixes this in five minutes.</li>
  <li><strong>Add or fix the local search basics.</strong> Show your address and phone number on every page, name your service area, and check your schema (hidden code that describes your business to Google). The <a href="/blog/google-business-profile-quarterly-checklist/">Google Business Profile checklist</a> is the right starting point.</li>
  <li><strong>Update the homepage headline.</strong> The first sentence has to name the trade, the city, and one specific reason to choose you. A generic tagline won't rank or bring in calls.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The rebuild path, when it is right</h2>

<p>If the test says "rebuild," the path is simpler:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Confirm what you need. Most service business rebuilds are eight to 12 pages, the same layout as the old site, rebuilt on tools that don't need constant upkeep.</li>
  <li>Pick the option that fits your budget: a subscription, a freelancer, an agency, or doing it yourself. Each costs a different amount over the site's life.</li>
  <li>Build it. Two to three weeks is a realistic time for a careful build of a typical service business site.</li>
</ol>
