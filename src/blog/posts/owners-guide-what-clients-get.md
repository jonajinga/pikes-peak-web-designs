<p>The day a site I built goes live, the client receives an Owner's Guide: a written reference for everything after launch. It covers who maintains what, how to ask for changes, and what counts as an emergency. It also explains the monthly invoice, how to recover the domain if a renewal lapses, where the site's search index lives, and how to reach me.</p>

<p>Most clients read only parts of it at first, then open it now and then for years. Having the answers in writing saves us both the "What did Jon say about X?" emails.</p>

<h2>Why a written guide rather than email follow-ups</h2>

<p>Three years into a typical working relationship, a client has 100 or more emails with me about the site. Finding "When does my domain renew?" in that thread takes five minutes of false starts. Finding it in the Owner's Guide takes 30 seconds.</p>

<p>The guide also serves the client's bookkeeper, a future developer if I retired, and the client's grown child who takes over the business. None of them will read the email thread.</p>

<p>Every client gets the same guide, with their own details added.</p>

<h2>The seventeen sections</h2>

<p>Each of the 17 sections answers a question that comes up while we work together.</p>

<p><strong>1. Who handles what.</strong> What I handle (build, hosting, security, content updates, monitoring) and what the client handles (domain renewal, the email account, the business itself). Naming the line keeps either of us from dropping something.</p>

<p><strong>2. How to ask for changes.</strong> There are four ways: the content-update form (preferred, because it organizes the request), email, phone or text (for urgent requests), and in person (rare, but possible for local clients). All four reach the same inbox.</p>

<p><strong>3. What counts as an emergency.</strong> The site is down, wrong contact details or a wrong price are live, there's a security concern, or the site has been vandalized. These skip the line and get handled the same day or the next business day.</p>

<p><strong>4. What does NOT count as an emergency.</strong> "I want to change the homepage photo" is a routine update, even if the client wants it today.</p>

<p><strong>5. How form submissions work.</strong> Submissions reach the client's business email through Web3Forms, a form-delivery service. Neither I nor Web3Forms stores them; they're forwarded at once, and the only copy is in the client's inbox.</p>

<p><strong>6. Your domain.</strong> The registrar (the company the domain is registered with), the name on the registration, the renewal date, and what happens if it lapses. It also explains how I watch the expiration date myself instead of relying on the registrar's reminder emails.</p>

<p><strong>7. Email setup.</strong> The client's email provider (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, or another), the MX records I set up (the settings that route your email), and how to add people to the email account.</p>

<p><strong>8. Hosting and uptime.</strong> Where the site is hosted (Cloudflare Pages, on my account), what that means in practice, and what happens during the rare hosting outage.</p>

<p><strong>9. Security.</strong> Why the way I build the site leaves little to attack: no database, no admin login page, and no PHP (the server code WordPress runs on). It also covers how the padlock certificate renews itself and what risks remain.</p>

<p><strong>10. SEO foundation.</strong> The work done at launch to help Google read the site: the sitemap, robots.txt, schema markup, meta tags, and share cards. It also covers what I keep watching and what the client can do in Google Search Console, Google's free site-report tool.</p>

<p><strong>11. Analytics.</strong> Cloudflare Web Analytics and Umami, the two visitor-statistics tools, what each measures, where to log in, and how to read the reports.</p>

<p><strong>12. Your Google Business Profile.</strong> How the website and the profile work together, the four-times-a-year profile checkup I recommend, and how to answer reviews.</p>

<p><strong>13. Adding new content.</strong> How to add a service page, a blog post, a service area, or new material on an existing page. Most are routine updates. Larger additions count as new pages, and a page beyond the ten included costs $100.</p>

<p><strong>14. Seasonal updates.</strong> The changes that come at the same time each year in each trade: the storm-season page in spring, holiday hours in November, and the year-in-review post in January. The guide records which ones the client wants and on what schedule.</p>

<p><strong>15. Your monthly invoice.</strong> What the $175 covers and doesn't, when invoices arrive, what happens if a payment fails, and how to update the payment method.</p>

<p><strong>16. Leaving the relationship.</strong> The exit terms in plain words. After the 12-month minimum, either of us can end the plan with 30 days' written notice. I help move your domain to a registrar of your choice and provide the analytics export, and the website goes offline at the end of the term. None of it is expensive or complicated, and the guide lists the steps.</p>

<p><strong>17. Quick contact card.</strong> Phone, email, the client portal link, and the four ways to reach me. Clients bookmark and use this page most.</p>

<h2>Why each section earns its keep</h2>

<p>A client's question prompted every section. I added Section 6 (your domain) after a client's domain expired during a year away and we both scrambled to recover it. I added Section 14 (seasonal updates) when I noticed I was sending three clients the same "Want me to add the storm-season page back?" email each May.</p>

<p>The guide changes as new questions come up, and sections that haven't helped anyone in two years come out. The 2026 version differs from the 2022 one, but every routine question still has a written answer.</p>

<h2>Where the guide lives</h2>

<p>The Owner's Guide lives at <a href="/owners-guide/" class="inline-link">a public address</a> on this site, with no login and one version for everyone. Each client's own details (their domain, email provider, and Google Business Profile address) go in a separate document I email at launch.</p>

<p>Keeping it public lets clients use it before, during, and after we work together, and lets anyone considering the service see what happens after launch. A private, per-client document would get lost. Every one I've seen in small-business work has: the email moved to a new provider, the document service was canceled, or the share link expired. A public page stays put.</p>

<h2>How clients use it</h2>

<p>Most clients skim it at launch, bookmark it, and come back three to five times a year with a specific question. That's the right use for a reference manual.</p>

<p>Clients who read it all at launch ask better questions later. They know the boundaries, the schedules, and what's billed by the hour, and their first 90 days go more smoothly.</p>

<h2>If your current relationship with a web vendor has no equivalent document</h2>

<p>That tells you something: the vendor either hasn't written down how things work after launch or doesn't share it. Ask, "What does the post-launch reference look like?" A vendor without a clear answer is running your site without a written plan.</p>

<p>For sites I build, the Owner's Guide is part of the standard plan, and the build isn't finished until I've sent it. The same idea works on any platform: a written reference, kept current, at an address the client can always find.</p>
