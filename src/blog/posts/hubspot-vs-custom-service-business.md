<p>HubSpot is one of the more sophisticated platforms for small and mid-sized businesses, and for some buyers it's the right answer. It bundles a website builder, a CRM (software that tracks customers and sales), marketing automation (automatic email campaigns and reports on how visitors become customers), sales tracking, and customer-service tools. For a business that needs all of them, the bundle is valuable.</p>

<p>For most of the service businesses I work with, though, the bundle is far bigger than the need, and so is the price. Here's what HubSpot does well, where it overshoots, and how the costs compare.</p>

<h2>What HubSpot is</h2>

<p>HubSpot is a sales platform built around "inbound marketing," which draws customers in with content. It began as a marketing-automation tool and grew into a CRM, website hosting, and customer-service tools. Today it covers most of the marketing and sales software a mid-sized business-to-business (B2B) company needs.</p>

<p>Its pitch is one connected platform instead of five subscriptions. For a buyer who uses all five, a website lead becomes a CRM contact, is assigned to a salesperson, gets an email sequence, and is tracked in one dashboard. Separate tools can do the same, but connecting them is a lot of work.</p>

<h2>What HubSpot does well</h2>

<p><strong>Everything connects.</strong> A lead's whole history (pages viewed, forms submitted, emails opened, notes from sales calls) sits on one contact record, and the reports cover the full customer journey without import scripts.</p>

<p><strong>The marketing automation is mature.</strong> Timed campaigns, actions triggered by visitor behavior, lead scoring (ranking leads by how likely they are to buy), A/B tests, and conversion reports match dedicated tools such as Pardot, Marketo, and ActiveCampaign.</p>

<p><strong>The CRM is capable.</strong> Sales tracking, deals, activity logs, and Gmail and Outlook integration compare well with dedicated CRMs such as Pipedrive, Close, and Zoho for small and mid-sized businesses.</p>

<p><strong>The platform is well supported.</strong> A large user community, thorough documentation, paid training, and certified partner consultants make help easy to hire.</p>

<h2>Where HubSpot overshoots for a typical service business</h2>

<p>The features that make HubSpot powerful are more than most small service businesses need:</p>

<p><strong>Most service businesses don't run drip campaigns.</strong> The plumber, the roofer, and the inspector get leads, turn them into jobs within hours or days, and move on. There's no long series of follow-up emails (a drip campaign); the job is booked or it isn't.</p>

<p><strong>Most service businesses don't need a CRM with stages.</strong> A typical week for a small service business brings 10 inquiries, eight phone quotes, and six booked jobs. That fits in an inbox or a notebook, and a CRM with sales stages and win probabilities is the wrong shape for the work.</p>

<p><strong>Most service businesses don't need conversion-funnel reports.</strong> The path is short: a visitor finds the site, calls or fills out the form, and becomes a customer. There are no multi-step interactions for that kind of reporting to analyze.</p>

<p><strong>Most service businesses don't need behavioral lead scoring.</strong> For a small service business, the lead score is "did they call?", and no pattern of behavior predicts better.</p>

<p>The features are capable, but a small service business pays for the whole bundle and uses 10 percent of it.</p>

<h2>The cost comparison</h2>

<p>HubSpot's 2026 pricing comes in tiers:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Free:</strong> a basic CRM, with no marketing automation and no custom domain on the website builder. Useful as a trial.</li>
  <li><strong>Starter ($20 to $30 a month per user):</strong> adds basic marketing automation and a custom domain, but it's limited to one user, and team collaboration requires an upgrade.</li>
  <li><strong>Professional ($800 to $1,800 a month, billed annually):</strong> full marketing automation, multiple users, and advanced reporting. This is where most HubSpot customers end up.</li>
  <li><strong>Enterprise ($3,200 or more a month):</strong> adds custom workflows, advanced permissions, and the features mid-sized companies need.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a small service business that wants a website, a contact form, and basic email, the Starter tier works but is limited. The Professional tier is what HubSpot's sales team pitches, and it comes to $9,600 to $21,600 a year.</p>

<p>The standard plan I offer is $175 a month with $0 down, or about $2,100 a year. HubSpot Professional costs five to 10 times as much, and the difference buys features most service businesses don't use.</p>

<h2>What the website piece looks like</h2>

<p>HubSpot's CMS Hub, the website-builder part of the platform, is capable. The templates look professional, the editor is comfortable, the search tools are built in, and pages load at an acceptable speed. As website builders go, it's near the top.</p>

<p>It isn't the fastest, the cheapest, or the easiest to move. A site hosted on HubSpot stays tied to HubSpot: the content can't be exported in a form that runs cleanly elsewhere, page addresses must follow HubSpot's structure, and leaving the platform is hard.</p>

<p>For a buyer who uses the rest of HubSpot, that tie is a benefit. For one who's there mainly for the website builder, it becomes a cost later.</p>

<h2>When HubSpot is the right answer</h2>

<p>I'd recommend HubSpot to three kinds of business:</p>

<p><strong>B2B companies with long sales cycles.</strong> For a software company, a professional-services firm with six-month sales cycles, or a consultancy that educates leads over time, the email sequences, lead scoring, and sales tracking do useful work.</p>

<p><strong>Businesses with a dedicated marketing person.</strong> If someone's job is running campaigns, A/B tests, and analysis of where leads come from, HubSpot earns its keep.</p>

<p><strong>Businesses already using three or more marketing tools.</strong> If the current setup is Mailchimp, ActiveCampaign, Pipedrive, Calendly, and a website builder, moving onto HubSpot may save money, even at the Professional tier.</p>

<p>For a service business that fits none of these, HubSpot is more than you need, and the standard plan fits better.</p>

<h2>The migration path</h2>

<p>For businesses that started on HubSpot and now pay for far more than they use, moving off takes some work, but the steps are clear:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Export the website content from HubSpot. CMS Hub allows it, but the HTML it produces is HubSpot-specific and needs cleanup.</li>
  <li>Export the CRM contacts as a CSV file (a standard spreadsheet format).</li>
  <li>Export form submissions and past reports.</li>
  <li>Build the new website on the standard plan.</li>
  <li>Move the CRM to a lighter tool if you need one. Most small service businesses don't, and a few do well with Pipedrive or with HubSpot's free tier kept as a contact list.</li>
  <li>Cancel the HubSpot subscription after a 30-day check period.</li>
</ol>

<p>The savings start right away and last, and the features you give up are usually ones you weren't using.</p>

<h2>If you are on HubSpot or evaluating it</h2>

<p>Ask which features you use. If the answer includes marketing automation, lead scoring, a multi-user CRM, and conversion reports, HubSpot is probably right for you. If it's "mostly the website and the contact form," there's a less expensive way to get those.</p>
