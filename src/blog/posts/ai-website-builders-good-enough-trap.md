<p>"Build your business website in 30 seconds with AI." That's the pitch from Wix AI, Squarespace AI, Hostinger AI, Durable, GoDaddy AIO, and a dozen other tools advertised on every podcast. You type a few words, and you get a website. It's impressive to watch.</p>

<p>An AI-built site looks like a website, with headers, sections, photos, and a contact form, all in the right places. It looks done. The trouble is what "looks done" leaves unmeasured. In local search for service businesses, a site can look done and still lose, and a losing site costs you customers without ever sending you the bill.</p>

<p>This post is about that gap: what "good enough" is, and what the distance to a custom site costs over a year.</p>

<h2>What an AI builder does</h2>

<p>The pitch is "AI generates your website." In practice, an AI builder takes a little information (your trade, name, and location), picks a template, fills it with generic text, and sometimes adds stock-style images. The result is a Wix or Squarespace site you didn't have to lay out yourself. The "AI" part is the auto-fill.</p>

<p>That has value. If you've put off a website because you don't know where to start, an AI starter gets you past the blank page. The mistake is confusing "I have a draft" with "I have a site that brings in customers."</p>

<h2>Five gaps in an AI-built site</h2>

<h3>1. Specificity</h3>

<p>AI builders learn from existing template text, so their copy reads like everyone else's. Your "About" page sounds like every other plumber's, and your service pages sound like every other electrician's. A customer scanning ten sites in five minutes notices, and Google's "helpful content" updates target this kind of writing directly. Generic copy ranks worse over time.</p>

<p>A custom site can do what an AI can't: speak in the owner's own voice. That means specific stories ("we did the drainage on the McKinley house last summer"), local knowledge ("anyone who has owned a home in Five Points knows the soil here"), and opinions ("we don't install [thing] because it never lasts in this climate"). That's the content that wins the click over a competitor's AI page.</p>

<h3>2. Page speed</h3>

<p>The AI label doesn't change the engine underneath. On Google's PageSpeed test, a 0 to 100 speed score, Wix AI sites score like Wix: 50&ndash;70 on mobile. Squarespace AI scores like Squarespace, 60&ndash;75. Hostinger AI performs like the Hostinger Builder underneath it. The AI that wrote your copy didn't rewrite the page builder's engine.</p>

<p>A custom-coded site scores 95&ndash;100 on mobile the day it launches, because speed is built into how it's made. Most of your visitors are on phones with spotty connections. They leave the slow site before its top image appears, and they stay on the fast one long enough to read the headline.</p>

<h3>3. Local SEO depth</h3>

<p>Local SEO (search engine optimization) is the work of showing up in local searches. AI builders get the details wrong. Their schema, the hidden labels that tell Google what a page is about, is rarely accurate. Their NAP (name, address, and phone number) often comes from outdated public data: an old number, a previous address, or a business name that's close to your Google Business Profile but not exact. Each mismatch weakens your ranking in Google's map results (the "local pack").</p>

<p>AI-made service-area pages also follow the "doorway page" pattern that Google's local ranking penalizes. The same paragraph appears again and again with a new city name, no local content, and no unique photos. A few of those pages can sink the whole site's local visibility.</p>

<p>A custom-coded site builds each service-area page with local content: your own photos, local references, and accurate schema. That's what Google rewards.</p>

<h3>4. Form intelligence</h3>

<p>Every AI-built contact form asks the same four things: name, email, phone, and message. It doesn't sort leads by trade, urgency, or job type. So the new-water-heater question lands beside "My basement is flooding right now" and "Do you do commercial work?"</p>

<p>A custom-coded form asks the right four or five questions: the kind of work, the timeline, the budget range, and the urgency. The form does the first round of sorting for you, which saves hours every week. The design alone changes how many people finish it: a form that asks "What's the problem?" with a list of options gets more completions than one with a blank "Message" box.</p>

<h3>5. Ownership</h3>

<p>An AI-built site is locked to the platform that made it, with no way to export it to another host. Leaving means starting over from a blank page. If the platform raises prices, changes its terms, drops a feature, or is sold, you have no say.</p>

<p>On my plan, I own the code while you're a client, and the site doesn't export either. What's yours is set out in a plain-English service agreement: your domain, registered in your name and under your control, plus your photos, text, and brand assets. If you cancel, I help you move the domain to a registrar you choose.</p>

<h2>The math: what does "good enough" cost?</h2>

<p>Here's a worked example for a typical local business. Say your average job is worth $500. A custom-coded site loads fast enough that one more mobile visitor a week calls instead of leaving. That's one more job a week, 50 more a year, and $25,000 more work. If your average job is $5,000, the number is ten times bigger.</p>

<p>That lost work never shows up on a bill. The owner of the AI-built site never sees the customers who almost called and then closed the tab. The owner sees only the leads that came in and assumes that's the most the site can do.</p>

<p>That's the trap: "good enough" tells you nothing. It can't rank first in your local pack or win the click over a sharper competitor, and the leads it misses never appear on a report. Owners find the cost only when they move to a custom site and see their leads double in three months. The new site isn't magic; the old one was leaving money on the floor.</p>

<h2>When an AI builder is the right call</h2>

<p>An AI builder is useful for a one-day research step, when you're still working out what you want. Make a free draft, see whether its rough shape fits, and use it for ideas. If the business is new and you're still six months from knowing whether it will last, an AI builder buys you time at little cost.</p>

<p>The mistake is treating that draft as the finished site. Once the business has steady traffic, revenue, and customers, the AI builder is leaving money on the table.</p>
