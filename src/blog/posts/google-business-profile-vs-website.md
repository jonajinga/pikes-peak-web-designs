<p>Owners often hear, from a past web designer or a friend in marketing, that "you don't need a website anymore, just a good Google Business Profile." Others hear the reverse: the profile is a side issue, and the website is what counts. Both are wrong. The two do different jobs, and a service business that wants to compete in local search needs both working well.</p>

<p>Here's what each one does, what each can't do, and why the advice to pick one is bad advice.</p>

<h2>What Google Business Profile is</h2>

<p>Google Business Profile (formerly Google My Business, and before that Google Places) is the free listing behind the business panel in Google search and the pin on Google Maps. The business owns it, Google hosts it, and it holds your name, address, phone, hours, service categories, photos, posts, and reviews.</p>

<p>Google's local ranking relies on the profile for three things:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Proximity.</strong> Google uses the profile's address to judge how close you are to the searcher. If it's wrong, you're missing from map results (the "local pack") where you should appear.</li>
  <li><strong>Category match.</strong> Google matches the search to your primary and secondary categories. On a search for "roofing," a roofer listed as "Roofing Contractor" beats one listed only as "General Contractor."</li>
  <li><strong>Reviews and ratings.</strong> Your star rating, review count, and how recent the reviews are all help decide who gets the top three map spots.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of these comes from your website. In map results, an excellent website with a missing or wrong profile loses to an ordinary website with a well-kept one.</p>

<h2>What Google Business Profile cannot do</h2>

<p>Within its limits the profile is excellent. Beyond them, the website takes over.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>It can't tell your story.</strong> It's a fill-in form: name, address, hours, categories, photos. There's no room to explain what makes you different, how you work, or what customers can expect, which is what moves someone from "this looks legitimate" to "I'm calling them now."</li>
  <li><strong>It can't explain pricing.</strong> Some categories allow a priced services list, but it's awkward, not offered everywhere, and can't explain trade-offs such as which package suits which job or what costs extra.</li>
  <li><strong>It can't capture leads your way.</strong> The "Contact" button starts a call or an email, with no form, booking calendar, or screening questions. In trades where those questions matter (HVAC sizing, roofing scope, electrical capacity), it captures only the bare minimum.</li>
  <li><strong>It can't rank for detailed questions.</strong> "Roofer in Colorado Springs" brings up map results, where a strong profile appears. "How long does an asphalt shingle roof last in Colorado Springs" brings up ordinary results, where it doesn't. A website that answers these longer, specific searches ("long-tail" searches) gets those visits.</li>
  <li><strong>It can be suspended without notice.</strong> The listing lives on Google's system, and most owners learn this only when it's flagged for review, often for unclear reasons, with nothing else online to fall back on.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What the website does that the profile cannot</h2>

<p>Once a customer decides you look plausible, the website answers the rest of their questions and takes them from "plausible" to "calling."</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>A presence Google doesn't control.</strong> The website lives on a domain registered in your name. Google can't suspend it, nobody else can edit it, and a platform's rule change can't make it vanish.</li>
  <li><strong>Search traffic for detailed questions.</strong> Every service page, city page, and well-researched article can rank for the exact question a customer types. Added up, that traffic from a well-built site equals or beats the profile's map traffic.</li>
  <li><strong>Content that builds trust.</strong> An about page, team bios, how you work, an FAQ, testimonials, and case studies close the gap between "I see them on Google" and "I trust them in my house." None of it fits in a profile.</li>
  <li><strong>Ways to turn visitors into customers.</strong> Forms with the right questions, online booking, tap-to-call and tap-to-text buttons, and payment links for customers who want to pay a deposit online.</li>
  <li><strong>Visitor statistics.</strong> The website shows where visitors came from, what they did, how many became leads, and how your marketing channels compare. The profile shows only totals.</li>
  <li><strong>Visibility that builds over time.</strong> Website content keeps ranking for years, while the profile's standing is recalculated constantly from recent activity. The website's visibility lasts and grows on itself.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How the two work together</h2>

<p>Done properly, the profile and the website are two views of the same business, and Google checks that they agree when it judges how prominent you are.</p>

<ul>
  <li>The name, address, and phone number (NAP) in the website footer match the profile exactly, down to how they're written.</li>
  <li>The website's <code>LocalBusiness</code> schema (code that states your business details for search engines) carries the same NAP, hours, and service categories as the profile.</li>
  <li>The website links to the profile, usually in a "Reviews" or "Find us on Google" section, which is a small but useful trust signal.</li>
  <li>The profile's website field points to your main website address, not a tracking link or a landing page.</li>
  <li>The photos on both look consistent, with the same branding and polish, so a customer who sees both gets one clear impression.</li>
</ul>

<p>Google's crawler (the program that reads websites for Google) compares the site's structured data with the profile side by side, and close agreement raises the prominence score behind your map ranking.</p>

<h2>What I do for every site I build</h2>

<p>On launch day, I check both:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Confirm the profile exists, the owner has claimed it, and its address, category, hours, and website fields are right.</li>
  <li>Confirm the website's footer, schema, and contact page match the profile exactly.</li>
  <li>If the profile lacks photos, suggest what to add.</li>
  <li>If the primary category is wrong (common when a business adds services over time), suggest the right one for the owner to set.</li>
  <li>Check that Google has indexed (added to its search catalog) the website's <code>/contact/</code> page and that its contact details match the profile.</li>
</ol>

<p>It takes about 30 minutes. From launch day on, the website and the profile support each other, and the ranking signals start to build.</p>

<h2>The takeaway</h2>

<p>A complete Google Business Profile gets you into the running in local search, and a well-built website turns that into calls. Skip either one and you give up visibility. The two complete each other, so run both and keep them consistent.</p>
