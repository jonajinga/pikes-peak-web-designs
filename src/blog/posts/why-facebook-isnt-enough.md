<p>It's a common pattern among small service businesses: a Facebook business page, maybe some Instagram posts, a Google Business Profile, and no website. The logic seems sound. Facebook is free, it's where people are, and it shows photos, contact details, and reviews. Why pay for a website?</p>

<p>Here's why that approach has a hard ceiling, and why the contractors who stay busy in competitive markets all have websites.</p>

<h2>Facebook Doesn't Rank on Google</h2>

<p>When someone searches Google for "electrician near me" or "roof repair [city]," Facebook pages barely show up. Google has its own set of services, including Google Business Profile, Google Search, and Google Maps, and it strongly favors websites built to rank in search.</p>

<p>A business without a website is invisible to anyone who starts with Google. And the data is clear: most people looking for a local service business start with a Google search, not on Facebook.</p>

<p>Facebook reaches people who already know you or who see your paid ads. Google is where people go when they need something right now and don't know who to call. Those are very different audiences, and the Google audience turns into customers at a much higher rate.</p>

<h2>You Don't Own Your Facebook Page</h2>

<p>Facebook can suspend your page with little explanation and little recourse. It can change its algorithm so your posts reach 3% of your followers instead of 30%. It can change its layout so your contact details are harder to find, or add a "Get Quotes" feature that puts competitors' ads on your page.</p>

<p>All of that has happened to service businesses that built their whole online presence on Facebook, and they had nothing to fall back on.</p>

<p>With your own website, your domain is registered in your name and you control it. Your content and your customer relationships stay with you, and Facebook can't suspend the site or change who sees it.</p>

<h2>Facebook Can't Match a Website's Credibility</h2>

<p>When a homeowner is choosing between two contractors for a $3,000 job, they look both up. One has a Facebook page with some job photos. The other has a professional website with a clear list of services, licensing details, detailed reviews, photos of their work, and an easy way to request a quote.</p>

<p>Which contractor seems more established and more trustworthy? Which seems more likely to be in business next year if something needs a warranty call?</p>

<p>A Facebook page alone suggests a small-time operation, while a professional website signals legitimacy. For high-ticket service work, that impression matters.</p>

<h2>Facebook's Organic Reach Has Collapsed</h2>

<p>Organic reach on Facebook, meaning how many of your followers see your posts without paid promotion, has fallen steadily for years. In 2012, a post on your business page might reach 16% of your followers. Today, that number is often 1% to 3%.</p>

<p>Facebook has deliberately limited organic reach to push businesses toward paid ads. If you rely on Facebook posts to reach new customers, you're on a platform that works against doing that for free.</p>

<p>A website that ranks well, by contrast, can keep sending you visitors from search for years, with no ongoing ad spending.</p>

<h2>Instagram and NextDoor Have the Same Problem</h2>

<p>The same goes for Instagram, NextDoor, and other social platforms. They're useful extras, not substitutes for a website.</p>

<p>Instagram doesn't rank on Google for service searches, and NextDoor is very local and hit-or-miss. Neither brings the search traffic that Google does.</p>

<p>Think of social media as a way to stay visible to people who already know you, and your website as the way to reach people who've never heard of you but need exactly what you do.</p>

<h2>Facebook and a Website Work Best Together</h2>

<p>A Facebook page is still worth keeping. Use it for:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Posting photos of finished jobs</li>
  <li>Taking part in your community and local groups</li>
  <li>Answering messages from potential customers</li>
  <li>Running targeted paid ads when you want a quick boost in calls</li>
</ul>

<p>But treat it as an addition to your website, not a replacement. Your website is where serious customers land and where Google sends people. It's what sets you apart from the hundreds of contractors who don't have one, or whose site doesn't work.</p>

<h2>The Cost Argument Falls Apart Fast</h2>

<p>The most common objection to a website is cost: "Facebook is free. A website costs money."</p>

<p>One extra customer a month from Google, whether a plumbing job, an HVAC repair, or a landscaping contract, likely pays for a whole year of a website. Most businesses that move from Facebook alone to a well-built website with local SEO see that return within the first 60 to 90 days.</p>

<p>So the question isn't whether a website costs money. It's whether the revenue it brings in is more than the cost, and for nearly every service business, it is.</p>
