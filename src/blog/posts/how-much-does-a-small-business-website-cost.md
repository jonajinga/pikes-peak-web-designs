<p>Ask this on Reddit, in a Facebook group, or on a trades forum, and you'll get a hundred versions of "it depends," each shaped by what the person replying sells. The numbers aren't hard to lay out, and the gap between sticker price and full cost is where most owners get burned.</p>

<p>Here's every realistic option in 2026, with its first-year and 10-year cost and where the hidden costs are.</p>

<h2>The five realistic options</h2>

<h3>1. DIY website builder (Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy, Weebly)</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Sticker price:</strong> $0 to start, then $16 to $49 a month.</li>
  <li><strong>First-year cost:</strong> $192 to $588 for the subscription, plus your time.</li>
  <li><strong>10-year cost:</strong> $1,920 to $5,880, if prices hold, which they almost never do.</li>
  <li><strong>Hidden costs:</strong> the 30 to 80 hours it usually takes to build it yourself, your time to keep it updated, and limits on speed and search ranking. There's also lock-in: you can't move the site to another host without rebuilding it.</li>
</ul>

<p>Builders make starting easy, and the plan is usually "I'll build it myself on a Saturday." One Saturday turns into six, and the result can still take three seconds to load on a phone, because the platform runs on general-purpose templates. If you're weighing a specific builder, the <a href="/vs-wix/">Wix comparison page</a> has the full breakdown.</p>

<h3>2. WordPress (self-hosted with a theme)</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Sticker price:</strong> $0 for the software, $5 to $25 a month for hosting, and $30 to $200 once for a theme (a ready-made design).</li>
  <li><strong>First-year cost:</strong> $400 to $1,200 if you build it yourself, or $1,500 to $5,000 if you hire someone to set it up.</li>
  <li><strong>10-year cost:</strong> $4,000 to $15,000, once you add plugin (add-on) licenses, security upkeep, theme updates, and the occasional "I need someone to fix the site" call.</li>
  <li><strong>Hidden costs:</strong> required security upkeep, plugin renewals, conflicts between plugins, and hosting upgrades as traffic grows. Expect a rebuild every three to four years, when the theme or the PHP version (the language WordPress runs on) loses support.</li>
</ul>

<p>WordPress is often pitched as cheap, and the first year is. By the seventh year it has needed more care than any other option here, and most owners aren't the right person to give it. The <a href="/vs-wordpress/">WordPress comparison</a> goes deeper.</p>

<h3>3. Freelance web designer (one-time project)</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Sticker price:</strong> $1,500 to $6,000 for the build.</li>
  <li><strong>First-year cost:</strong> $1,500 to $6,000, plus $200 to $500 for hosting.</li>
  <li><strong>10-year cost:</strong> $4,500 to $15,000 if you leave the site untouched (most people don't), or another $2,000 to $5,000 every few years to refresh it.</li>
  <li><strong>Hidden costs:</strong> the freelancer moves on, you need someone else for changes, nobody else knows the code, and the site goes stale.</li>
</ul>

<p>A freelancer is the easiest option in year one and the hardest by year three: it sells well and ages poorly. The <a href="/vs-freelancer/">freelancer comparison</a> makes the full case.</p>

<h3>4. Traditional agency</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Sticker price:</strong> $5,000 to $20,000 for the build.</li>
  <li><strong>First-year cost:</strong> $7,000 to $25,000, once monthly retainers, ad-management fees, and "phase 2" extras are added.</li>
  <li><strong>10-year cost:</strong> $30,000 to $100,000 or more, depending on the retainer and how often the site is rebuilt.</li>
  <li><strong>Hidden costs:</strong> retainers that creep upward, a rebuild every three years, handoffs as your account passes through five people, and waiting to be "scheduled in" for changes that should take an afternoon.</li>
</ul>

<p>Agencies do excellent work for clients who can absorb the price and need a full staff. For small service businesses, the math almost never works. The <a href="/vs-agencies/">agency comparison</a> covers it in detail.</p>

<h3>5. Subscription web design (the model on this site)</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Sticker price:</strong> $175 a month, $0 down, with a 12-month minimum, then month to month with 30 days' written notice.</li>
  <li><strong>First-year cost:</strong> $2,100, all in, covering hosting, SSL (the padlock that secures your site), monitoring, unlimited content updates, a blog, and the on-page search basics. There's nothing else to buy.</li>
  <li><strong>10-year cost:</strong> $21,000 if the rate stays flat, with the same site and the same person the whole time, and no rebuild fees or retainer creep.</li>

</ul>

<h2>What you are paying for</h2>

<p>These aren't five versions of the same thing. They're five different products:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Builders</strong> sell you software, and you do the work.</li>
  <li><strong>WordPress</strong> sells you a platform, and you and your hired help do the work.</li>
  <li><strong>Freelancers</strong> sell you a one-time project, and you handle everything after it.</li>
  <li><strong>Agencies</strong> sell you a staff, and you pay its overhead for as long as you work together.</li>
  <li><strong>A subscription</strong> sells you the whole job, and the person who builds the site runs it for as long as you keep it.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of these suits everyone. A solo blogger doesn't need a $175-a-month subscription, and neither does a national software company. Most small service businesses are different: roofers, inspectors, electricians, plumbers, HVAC companies, landscapers, and painters. For them, a subscription is the only option with reasonable costs in year one and year 10, and nothing breaking in between.</p>

<h2>How to pick</h2>

<p>Three filters:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>If the website is a hobby and leads don't matter</strong>, a DIY builder is fine, and nothing in this post applies.</li>
  <li><strong>If the website is the front door of a service business</strong>, the cheapest option is almost always the most expensive over its lifetime. Spend a little more on something that doesn't need babysitting.</li>
  <li><strong>If you're not sure which group you're in</strong>, a <a href="/contact/">free call</a> (20 to 30 minutes on Google Meet) will settle whether your current site is worth saving.</li>
</ol>

<p>So the cost of a small business website isn't one number. It's what you pay this year compared with what you pay over the life of the site, and anyone who quotes only year one is selling year one. Ask every vendor what it looks like at year five, and the answers will sort the sound options from the weak ones.</p>
