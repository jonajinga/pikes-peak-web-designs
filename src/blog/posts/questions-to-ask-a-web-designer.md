<p>Most service business owners hire a web designer the way they hire a contractor: they ask around, pick the friend-of-a-friend with the best portfolio, sign, and hope. When that fails, it's expensive: a site that took six months to launch, a designer who vanished afterward, code locked in the designer's account, or a bill far past the quote.</p>

<p>Seven questions, asked early, show which designers will help your business. Each has a clear right answer and a clear wrong one, and how the designer responds often tells you more than the words.</p>

<h2>1. "Who writes the code?"</h2>

<p><strong>Right answer:</strong> "I do" (for a freelancer) or "the lead developer on the team, who you'll meet on the first call" (for an agency).</p>

<p><strong>Wrong answer:</strong> "We have a development partner" (vague), "It's outsourced overseas" (cheap, with the usual results), or any answer that doesn't name a specific person.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> that person shapes the site for the next ten years, and if you can't name them, you can't reach them later. <a href="/vs-agencies/">Most agency builds</a> pass through three to five people, the plan gets distorted at every hand-off, and in the end nobody can fully explain the code.</p>

<h2>2. "What stack will the site be built on?"</h2>

<p><strong>Right answer:</strong> a specific set of tools (the "stack") with a reason. "Custom-coded with Eleventy, because it's fast and you can host it on Cloudflare for almost nothing" is a good answer. "WordPress with the X theme, because you want a drag-and-drop editor" is fine if it matches what you need.</p>

<p><strong>Wrong answer:</strong> "Whatever you want," "We work in everything," or any answer that shows no opinion. A designer without one hasn't built enough sites to form one.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> the stack sets what the site costs over its life. <a href="/blog/wordpress-vs-custom-code/">WordPress is cheap to start and expensive to maintain</a>, and custom code is the reverse. Wix and Squarespace are inexpensive at both ends but limit how fast the site can be and how well it can rank. No answer is right for everyone, but the designer should be able to explain why theirs fits your business.</p>

<h2>3. "What happens if I want to leave?"</h2>

<p><strong>Right answer:</strong> the domain is registered in your name, and you control it. The exit terms are in writing before you sign: how much notice you give, what happens to the domain, and what happens to the site. Arrangements differ: some designers hand over the code. On my monthly plan, I own the code, which is how I can build the site with $0 down. When you cancel, I help move your domain to a registrar of your choice, and the website goes offline at the end of the term. Either arrangement is fair if it's written down and you know it before you start.</p>

<p><strong>Wrong answer:</strong> "We'll register the domain for you" (it ends up in their name), "We'll sort that out when the time comes," or any exit terms that aren't in writing. This is the question most likely to expose a designer selling a contract trap instead of a service.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> the relationship will end someday. The designer retires, the agency closes, the price changes, the quality drops, or your business outgrows them, so the exit terms matter more than the entry terms. Read the agreement before you sign. If there's no agreement, that's a red flag in itself.</p>

<h2>4. "How fast will the site load on a phone?"</h2>

<p><strong>Right answer:</strong> a specific number, with a way to measure it. "Under two seconds, with a score above 90 on Google's PageSpeed test (rated 0 to 100)" is a good answer. "Under one second, on phones and desktops, and I can show you live tests on sites I've built" is a great answer.</p>

<p><strong>Wrong answer:</strong> "It'll be fast," "We don't measure that," or "PageSpeed doesn't mean much." All three are excuses. <a href="/blog/why-page-speed-matters/">Page speed is the most important factor in turning visitors into calls</a> on a service business site. A designer who won't commit to a number doesn't know what their sites will do.</p>

<h2>5. "What is the total first-year cost, in writing?"</h2>

<p><strong>Right answer:</strong> one number that covers design, build, hosting, the security certificate, maintenance, and any recurring fees. "$2,100 a year, all in, and here's the breakdown" is a good answer.</p>

<p><strong>Wrong answer:</strong> a build price without the ongoing costs ("$5,000 for the build, hosting is separate"), or "it depends on what you need" with no range offered. This is where monthly charges creep in.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> in most cases, the build is the smaller part of what a site costs over its life. <a href="/blog/how-much-does-a-small-business-website-cost/">A separate post</a> works through the numbers. Only a full first-year figure lets you compare like with like.</p>

<h2>6. "How will I make changes after launch?"</h2>

<p><strong>Right answer:</strong> a specific process. "Email me, and I'll make the change quickly on most business days, included in the monthly fee" is a good answer. "There's a dashboard you can edit from, and I'll help with anything beyond that" is fine if you want to make your own edits.</p>

<p><strong>Wrong answer:</strong> "We bill changes at $X an hour" (unusual outside agencies), "You'll need to learn WordPress" (only if you want to), or any answer that makes routine changes painful. The site will need changes every month for ten years, and the costs add up.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> hard-to-get changes are the main reason owners let their websites go stale. If an update takes a sales call, an estimate, an invoice, and a two-week wait, it won't happen, and a site that isn't updated stops earning.</p>

<h2>7. "Can I call three of your clients?"</h2>

<p><strong>Right answer:</strong> names with phone numbers or emails, shared with the clients' permission. A designer who has done good work for service businesses for a while has clients who'll speak well of them.</p>

<p><strong>Wrong answer:</strong> "I can't share that for privacy reasons," "Most of our clients prefer not to be contacted," or any answer that turns a routine reference request into something complicated. References are normal for any other service business, and web design is no different.</p>

<p><strong>Why it matters:</strong> the portfolio shows the work. References show what the designer is like to work with, and over six months or six years, that matters more than how the portfolio looks.</p>

<h2>Putting it together</h2>

<p>A designer worth hiring answers all seven clearly and specifically. The warning signs are usually obvious: vague answers, defensiveness, a turn to selling, an upsell you didn't ask for, or a question back ("What's your budget?") before they've answered yours. The questions take about 15 minutes, and they can save years of trouble.</p>
